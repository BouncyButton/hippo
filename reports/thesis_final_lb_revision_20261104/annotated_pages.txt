Abstract
Semantic segmentation assigns a class to each location in an image, allowing structures
such as organs to be identified and measured. In medical imaging, it supports the study of
anatomy and disease, but obtaining annotations and training accurate models require time
and resources. This thesis investigates whether spatial information already contained in
annotations can improve three-dimensional segmentation when labelled data and training
computation are limited.
The approach uses Logic Tensor Networks (LTNs), which combine neural learning with
knowledge expressed as logical statements, and their logarithmic formulation, logLTN.
Four additional training objectives encourage correct labels near boundaries and correct
label transitions between neighbouring locations, weight errors by distance from class
boundaries, and balance supervision across layers of a volume. These objectives reuse
existing annotations without requiring additional manual labelling.
The medical experiments concern hippocampus segmentation, relevant to the study of
memory and Alzheimer’s disease, using the Medical Segmentation Decathlon (MSD) and
Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) datasets. Salt-body segmentation in
synthetic GeoFWI3D volumes tests whether the benefits extend beyond medical imaging.
Experiments cover four architectures trained from scratch under matched computational
budgets within each dataset, and adaptation of pretrained Segment Anything Model 3
(SAM3) and its medical specialization, MedSAM3, using small labelled support sets.
For every architecture and dataset studied from scratch, at least one tested combination
improves mean validation Dice, a measure of segmentation overlap, over the matched
baseline. The largest gains are 11.97 percentage points in the medical experiments and 6.14
points in the geological experiments. The best tested adaptation configurations improve
Dice by 3.45 to 5.38 points. The preferred combination varies across models and tasks,
and adding more constraints does not consistently improve performance. These findings
support annotation-derived spatial supervision as a useful training strategy under limited
resources, with benefits demonstrated in both medical and geological segmentation.

A signed-distance field assigns each location a distance from the annotated boundary,
negative inside the region and positive outside. Kervadecet al.use this field to weight
predicted probabilities throughout the image [20]. This supplies a geometric learning
signal without extracting a discrete predicted contour. The classwise distance term in
this thesis follows that approach; its exact distance convention and loss are developed in
Section 3.4.
Anatomically Constrained Neural Networks supervise segmentation through a learned
representation of valid shapes [21]. clDice instead compares masks and their skeletons,
thin centreline representations, to encourage connected paths in tubular structures [22].
These approaches concern properties that local membership and transition agreement do
not enforce. The present edge term preserves the changes in the annotation, including its
boundaries; it does not require a globally connected prediction.
Bergaminet al.use LTNs for MSD hippocampus segmentation with relations concerning
whether regions are connected, whether one is contained in another, and their relative
volumes [23]. Their work provides a direct predecessor for logical supervision of this
task. The present study evaluates four families of local or classwise geometric supervision
across multiple architectures under an optimization FLOP ceiling. It also examines the
compatibility of simplified anatomical rules with the actual label maps (Appendix B.5).
Together, these studies motivate explicit spatial supervision while showing that different
objectives encode different properties. The fourteen-objective comparison evaluates the
selected families and their combinations; the literature methods provide context rather
than further methods tested in these experiments. Differences in datasets and protocols
preclude a numerical ranking against those published systems.
2.5 Pretrained Segmentation and Low-Rank Adaptation
Pretraining changes the starting point of learning. A target model can specialize repre-
sentations acquired from a larger training collection, so the relevant resource question
becomes the additional data and optimization needed for adaptation. A prompt is an
additional input, such as a point or a text description, specifying what to segment in
an image. Fine-tuning instead changes model parameters using labelled examples. A
pretrained segmentation system can use both.

interface. An inner band encourages the prediction to retain foreground close to that
interface, while an outer band discourages extension into adjacent background. The
selected locations depend on the reference mask, so an incorrect predicted contour cannot
move the target support away from an error.

3.6 Combined Objective and Experimental Scope
Training retains the model’s usual segmentation loss and adds the selected spatial penalties.
Each penalty has a coefficient that controls its strength relative to the other terms. An
activation schedule determines when it contributes and can increase its contribution
gradually during training. These choices let the usual class supervision and the additional
spatial guidance contribute to the same parameter update.

Complementary domains motivate combinations, but do not ensure complementary
gradients. Terms can reinforce or oppose one another, and their computation consumes
part of a fixed optimization allowance. The coefficients are set by calibration, which
compares the losses’ gradients on training examples. The experiments treat this calibration,
activation schedules and resource accounting as parts of the tested configuration, specified
in Chapter 4.
The task-specific domains, crop conventions and sampled reductions are specified
with the experimental protocols in Chapter 4. These choices matter when transferring a
constraint between datasets. At inference, the network uses its image and any specified
prompts; reference-derived spatial targets are needed only during training.

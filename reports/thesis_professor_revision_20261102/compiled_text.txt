1     Introduction
Three-dimensional semantic segmentation identifies structures in an image by assigning
a class to every voxel, the three-dimensional counterpart of a pixel. A stack of medical
image slices can thereby become a map of an organ; a geological image can become a map
of a subsurface formation. These maps make it possible to measure the size of a structure
and study its shape and relationship to its surroundings. Segmentation thus connects
image acquisition with quantitative analysis.
    Traditionally, reference segmentations have been produced by delineating structures
in the images. A segmentation mask stores these decisions as class labels on the image
grid. In supervised learning, a neural network learns to reproduce such masks from paired
images and annotations. Convolutional encoder–decoder models, notably U-Net and its
volumetric extension, made this approach widely applicable to biomedical images [13, 12].
Transformer-based models offer another way to combine information across an image,
while pretrained segmentation models allow an existing representation to be adapted to a
new task. These developments provide several routes to automatic segmentation, but they
do not remove two practical constraints:

    • Limited annotations. Delineating a structure throughout a volume takes time and
      can require specialist knowledge. In medical imaging, uncertain boundaries further
      complicate consistent labelling [1].

    • Limited computation. Training a volumetric model can be expensive. An approach
      that reaches useful accuracy sooner may be valuable even when a longer training
      run would eventually close the gap.

    This thesis asks whether the spatial information already contained in a mask can make
learning more effective under these restrictions. Consider a voxel just inside an annotated
object: its label specifies membership, but its location also tells us that it lies close to
the object’s edge. Two neighbouring voxels reveal whether the annotation changes class
at that location. The proposed approach gives these relationships an explicit role in the
training objective, alongside the model’s usual segmentation loss. It needs no additional
manual annotations.
    Logic Tensor Networks (LTNs) provide a way to express such supervision [8]. They
belong to neuro-symbolic artificial intelligence, which combines neural learning with
explicitly stated knowledge. In an LTN, a statement such as “these annotated inner
voxels belong to the object” receives a numerical degree of satisfaction from the model’s
predictions. Training can then increase that satisfaction. The logarithmic formulation,
logLTN, represents truth values through their logarithms and allows the corresponding


                                             3
penalties to be evaluated in that space [9]. Here, spatial constraints means soft training
objectives: they encourage the stated properties without guaranteeing them in every
predicted segmentation.
   Four forms of supervision are studied. The first reinforces membership on either side of
an annotated boundary. The second encourages neighbouring predictions to reproduce the
class transitions in the mask. The third uses distance from each class boundary to weight
membership errors. The fourth balances class supervision across spatial layers of the
annotated region. The thesis connects each property to its logical statement and numerical
loss, then tests the terms individually and in combination. This separates the formulation
of a useful constraint from the empirical question of whether it improves learning.
   The investigation first evaluates these ideas on salt-body segmentation in GeoFWI3D,
a collection of synthetic subsurface models and associated reflectivity volumes [2]. The
foreground is the salt region and the background is the surrounding material. This task
provides a nonmedical setting for testing spatial supervision before its application to
anatomical images.
   The medical application is hippocampus segmentation in magnetic resonance imaging
(MRI). The hippocampus is involved in memory and is affected early in Alzheimer’s disease;
identifying its boundaries supports measurements of anatomical volume and variation
[3]. Two datasets provide complementary tasks. The Medical Segmentation Decathlon
(MSD) distinguishes anterior and posterior hippocampus from background [4]. It therefore
requires both the outer contour and an internal division to be recovered. The Alzheimer’s
Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) supplies images for a binary task, distinguishing
hippocampus from background in separate left and right crops [7]. Figure 1 illustrates the
images and masks used in these three settings.
   The experiments address two learning situations. Models trained from scratch must
learn from the available task annotations, whereas pretrained models start with repre-
sentations learned from other images. The first comparison includes convolutional and
transformer architectures to test whether the usefulness of spatial supervision depends on
how a model represents image context. The second adapts Segment Anything Model 3
(SAM3) to geological images and its medical specialization, MedSAM3, to hippocampus
images [26, 10]. It asks whether spatial supervision still helps when the initial representa-
tion is already available. Within each study, constrained and baseline training share the
data and the stated computational or update budget.
   The contribution is a common LTN/logLTN formulation of four spatial objectives,
together with an experimental assessment across these architectures, datasets and learning
situations. An additional audit checks whether simplified geometric interpretations of
the MSD annotation protocol agree with the stored masks. The results examine both


                                             4
the improvements and the settings in which an added term has little benefit or reduces
accuracy. Figure 2 summarizes the design; the training schedules and replication details
are given in Chapter 4.

Thesis organization. Chapter 2 introduces the concepts needed to formulate the
spatial objectives, which are developed in Chapter 3. Chapter 4 defines the datasets and
experimental procedures. Chapter 5 presents and interprets the geological comparison,
MSD and ADNI, followed by SAM3 adaptation on GeoFWI3D and MedSAM3 adaptation
on MSD and ADNI. Error patterns, metric sensitivity and limitations complete the analysis.
The limitations include an annotation audit explaining the scope for anatomical constraints.
Chapter 6 closes the thesis and sets out future work. Technical derivations, extended
numerical results and reproducibility records are collected in the appendices.

2.1     Segmentation and Supervised Objectives
A digital image is an array of measurements. In a two-dimensional image, each entry
is a pixel; in a volume, each entry is a voxel. Multiple channels can represent colour or
different imaging measurements, although the datasets used here have one input channel.
Image classification assigns a label to an entire image, object detection locates objects, and
semantic segmentation assigns a class at every location. Segmentation therefore preserves
the spatial information needed to describe a region’s size and shape.
    For an image volume indexed by i (a case), let Ωi = {0, . . . , Di − 1} × {0, . . . , Hi − 1} ×
{0, . . . , Wi − 1} be the voxel grid. The image xi : Ωi → RM contains M measurements
at each location, and the reference map yi : Ωi → C assigns one of K mutually exclusive
classes. The subscript i allows native image dimensions to vary across volumes; individual
training recipes may prepare a fixed input size. Background has label zero. The one-hot
value yi,c (v) = 1{yi (v) = c} indicates whether voxel v belongs to class c. A network with
parameters θ produces logits zθ,i,c (v), which are unrestricted class scores. The softmax
operation converts them to nonnegative probabilities that sum to one:

                                       exp(zθ,i,c (v))        X
                     pθ,i,c (v) = P                       ,         pθ,i,c (v) = 1.             (1)

Training operates on these continuous outputs. The final label is ybi (v) = arg maxc∈C pθ,i,c (v).
    The class vocabulary determines which errors a constraint can detect. Salt segmentation
uses C = {0, S}, where S denotes salt. Binary hippocampus segmentation uses {0, H},
where H denotes hippocampus. MSD uses {0, A, P }: A and P denote anterior and
posterior hippocampus, respectively. Its whole-foreground probability is qi = pθ,i,A + pθ,i,P .
A term depending only on this sum cannot distinguish an anterior/posterior exchange that
preserves it. Furthermore, foreground thresholding and multiclass argmax are distinct
decision rules: for (p0 , pA , pP ) = (0.40, 0.35, 0.25), the foreground sum exceeds 0.5 although
background is the most probable individual class. The experiments use multiclass argmax
before evaluating the labelled regions.

2.2    Segmentation Architectures
The four architectures provide different ways to use the surrounding image when classifying
a voxel. A convolutional model builds context through repeated local operations; a
transformer can combine features from more distant locations through attention. Testing
both helps assess whether a spatial objective is useful beyond a single model family. All
four connect an encoder, which extracts image features, to a decoder, which converts
those features into voxelwise predictions. The comparison also includes two convolutional
baselines: a compact U-Net and the more elaborate nnU-Net training pipeline. It therefore
probes sensitivity to both representation and training recipe, rather than isolating encoder
architecture alone.
   U-Net builds increasingly coarse feature maps through an encoder and combines them
with an upsampling decoder through skip connections [13]. Coarse features provide context,
while the skips retain information useful for localization. Three-dimensional U-Net applies
these operations across a volume [12]. The compact implementation in this study provides
a convolutional reference with a relatively small configured network.
   nnU-Net uses a U-Net architecture within a system that configures preprocessing,
network design and training for the segmentation problem [15]. Its identity therefore

                                              10
includes the training pipeline as well as the encoder. The present experiment fixes a
particular configuration and retains its native supervised loss and deep supervision. This
makes the constrained and unconstrained versions comparable within that system.
   Transformer encoders use self-attention to combine token features through input-
dependent query–key similarities [16]. UNETR (U-Net with a transformer encoder) divides
the volume into patches, embeds them as a sequence and applies global attention on a
fixed token grid [17]. Features from intermediate blocks are reshaped and upsampled for a
convolutional decoder. Attention can connect distant patches within the supplied crop,
although the crop itself still limits the available context.
   SwinUNETR combines a shifted-window (Swin) transformer with a U-Net decoder,
forming a hierarchy of transformer features [19]. Attention is restricted to local windows;
alternating shifted partitions exchange information across their boundaries, and patch
merging produces coarser representations [18]. The resulting feature maps feed a convolu-
tional decoder. Figure 4 contrasts this hierarchy with UNETR’s fixed token grid and the
convolutional U-Net organization.

2.3        Logic Tensor Networks and logLTN
Logic Tensor Networks combine neural predictions with logical statements about the
desired output. Their numerical interpretation, called a grounding, maps a statement to a
truth degree in [0, 1]; this map is denoted by Gθ . It makes statements differentiable with
respect to the model parameters [8]. Individuals are represented by constants or variables,
predicates evaluate their properties in [0, 1], and numerical operators combine these truth
degrees. A segmentation predicate can take a voxel and its image as an individual and use
the model’s class probability as its grounding:
                                                    
                                      Gθ Segc (i, v) = pθ,i,c (v).                               (6)

Reference indicators and annotation-derived geometry provide fixed information during
an update. The network supplies the trainable values. The satisfaction of a compound
statement depends on how its component truth degrees are combined. It measures
agreement with the chosen rule; it is not automatically the probability that the entire rule
is true.
   logLTN carries out the corresponding operations in logarithmic truth space [9]. If
ℓθ (ϕ) = log Gθ (ϕ), complete satisfaction has value zero and lower satisfaction has a negative
value. Product conjunction becomes addition. For the objectives in this thesis, universal
reduction takes the weighted mean of local log-truths:

                                      X                                        X
            ℓθ (∀ω u ∈ U : P (u)) =         ω(u)ℓθ (P (u)),       ω(u) > 0,          ω(u) = 1.   (7)
                                      u∈U                                      u∈U


Uniform weights yield the ordinary mean; exponentiation then gives the geometric mean
of the local truths. This normalization prevents a larger domain from increasing the
loss solely because it contains more individuals. The particular weights and domain still
determine how strongly each location contributes.
   Geometric errors can enter the same framework. Any nonnegative violation eθ (u)
defines an agreement predicate through

                    Gθ (Cons(u)) = exp(−eθ (u)),              ℓθ (Cons(u)) = −eθ (u).            (8)




                                                    12
The negative mean log-truth is then exactly the mean violation. The implementation
evaluates that violation directly, avoiding a redundant exponential followed by a logarithm.
This provides a common description of probability-based membership and geometric
penalties.
   An exact correspondence requires the same groundings, domains, weights and aggre-
gation order in the logical expression and the implemented loss. When those operations
agree, so do their gradients. Logical notation therefore clarifies what the model is asked to
satisfy; empirical comparisons establish whether that supervision helps. These constraints
remain soft objectives and do not guarantee satisfaction on the final discrete prediction.


2.4    Related Work on Spatial Supervision
Prior work incorporates spatial information through location-dependent weights, geometric
penalties and representations of valid shape. Ronneberger et al. weighted cross-entropy
near boundaries separating touching cells [13]. This demonstrates how an annotation can
specify where class supervision deserves extra emphasis. The boundary bands studied
here use explicit inner and outer domains, with balanced side reductions. In the principal
experiment, those domains follow the exterior of the whole foreground.
   Kervadec et al. formulate boundary supervision as a regional integral of predicted
probabilities against a reference signed-distance field [20]. The formulation supplies a
geometric learning signal without extracting a hard predicted contour. The classwise
distance term in this thesis follows that approach, with specified discrete distances, clipping
and normalization. A constant shift makes its loss nonnegative while preserving the model
gradient.
   Anatomically Constrained Neural Networks supervise segmentation through a learned
representation of valid shapes [21]. clDice instead uses mask and skeleton overlap to
address connectivity in tubular structures [22]. These approaches concern properties
that local membership and transition agreement do not enforce. The present edge term
preserves the changes in the annotation, including its boundaries; it does not require a
globally connected prediction.
   Bergamin et al. use LTNs for MSD hippocampus segmentation with relations concerning
connection, non-nesting and relative volume [23]. Their work provides a direct predecessor
for logical supervision of this task. The present study evaluates four families of local or
classwise geometric supervision across multiple architectures under an optimization FLOP
ceiling. It also examines the compatibility of candidate anatomical proxies with the actual
label maps (Section 5.5.1).
   Together, these studies motivate explicit spatial supervision while showing that different
objectives encode different properties. The fourteen-objective comparison evaluates the

                                              13
selected families and their combinations; the literature methods provide context rather
than further experimental arms. Differences in datasets and protocols preclude a numerical
ranking against those published systems.


2.5    Pretrained Segmentation and Low-Rank Adaptation
Pretraining changes the starting point of learning. A target model can specialize represen-
tations acquired from a larger training collection, so the relevant resource question becomes
the additional data and optimization needed for adaptation. Prompting specifies the target
of an individual prediction; fine-tuning changes parameters using labelled examples. A
pretrained segmentation system can use both.
   The Segment Anything Model (SAM) combines an image encoder, a prompt encoder
and a mask decoder, using prompts such as points or bounding boxes [24]. MedSAM
adapts this framework to medical images with box prompts [25]. SAM3 supports concepts
specified through text or exemplars [26], and MedSAM3 specializes that capability for
medical concepts [10]. The hippocampus experiments use fixed anatomical phrases, and
the geological SAM3 experiment uses the phrase salt body. These queries are shared across
cases; inference receives no case-specific localization from a held-out reference mask.
   Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) parameterizes the update to a selected weight matrix
through two smaller trainable factors [27]:

                                   α
                    Weff = W0 +      BA,       A ∈ Rr×din ,   B ∈ Rdout ×r ,                  (9)
                                   r

The base matrix W0 is frozen, r is the rank and α controls the update scale. The factors
contain r(din + dout ) parameters, compared with din dout in the dense matrix. The rank
restriction applies to the update, while the pretrained matrix remains available in the
forward computation.
   Optimizing fewer parameters reduces gradient and optimizer-state storage. It still
requires execution of the underlying model and propagation of gradients to the adapter
locations. Parameter count, memory and runtime therefore describe different aspects of
adaptation effort. MedSAM3 continues a released medical adapter, whereas SAM3 starts
with fresh factors whose initial product BA is zero. Both studies optimize 18,497,664
adapter parameters and restore the same starting state before each matched run. Their
initial representations differ, while their paired comparisons isolate the effect of the training
objective within each study. Sections 4.6 and 4.6 specify the semantic outputs, support
data and adaptation schedules.

3     Spatial Constraints in the LTN/logLTN Framework
The four objectives developed here extract different learning signals from the same reference
mask. Bands emphasize membership near its boundary; edges compare neighbouring
transitions; signed distances describe errors relative to individual classes; and depth
weights redistribute class supervision across spatial layers. Their definitions specify both
the property to encourage and the locations on which it is evaluated.

4.1    Design and Units of Comparison
A fold identifies a data partition and a seed controls training randomness. Repeating seeds

also changes the support and evaluation images. These repetitions provide evidence about
consistency under the available data, while sharing parts of the same experimental setting.

admissible state in the capped experiments, the final 200-update MedSAM3 adapter and
the final 25-update SAM3 adapter, without validation-selected checkpoint replacement or
early stopping.

objective and adds boundary-band supervision; the same convention applies to combina-

model-specific protocols below.

4.2.1   Frozen Model Recipes and Spatial Geometry

The model recipes preserve batch size one and bfloat16 autocast through NVIDIA’s
CUDA parallel-computing platform with float32 parameters. nnU-Net uses its native
Dice-plus-cross-entropy objective and deep supervision. SwinUNETR and the compact
U-Net use Dice with background included. Geological UNETR uses foreground Dice plus
cross-entropy, squared prediction terms in Dice, dropout 0.1 and learning rate 6 × 10−4 .
   In preliminary experiments, the initial UNETR recipe consistently struggled to achieve
satisfactory validation performance across the seeds tested within the fixed FLOP budget.
Two modifications were introduced during development to improve its generalization on
GeoFWI3D: horizontal data augmentation and exclusion of the observation frame from
the spatial constraints. Both address properties of this geological task and were retained
in its final UNETR training recipe.
   The augmentation policy selects among eight transformations in the horizontal (XY)
plane: rotations of 0◦ , 90◦ , 180◦ or 270◦ , each with or without a reflection. Each trans-
formation is applied jointly to the image and its reference mask, preserving the depth
(Z) axis and their alignment. These operations rearrange voxels without interpolation
or intensity changes and are applied only during training. The policy is shared by the
UNETR baseline and all its constrained objectives.
   The second modification excludes the outer two voxel layers from the band and edge
supports. A salt body can continue beyond the observed cube, whereas zero-padded
erosion can treat the cube face as a region boundary. Exclusion is intended to concentrate
auxiliary supervision on interfaces observed within the volume, reducing its dependence
on where the observation window ends. It changes the support of the constraints while
preserving the full input, prediction grid and baseline segmentation loss. Its development
comparison is reported in Section 5.1; the modification is a task-specific loss-design choice
rather than a requirement of the UNETR architecture.
   All four models use two-step, six-neighbour bands on binary salt membership. The
other three geological recipes retain zero-padded morphology at the cube faces. All four
use stabilized band denominators and require both sides to be nonempty. Appendix A.4
specifies the reductions. The final comparison therefore evaluates complete model recipes,

4.2.2   Calibration, Scheduling and Compute Allowance

The geological study uses a larger computation allowance because its inputs are larger.
The reference is the original measured cost of 152 nnU-Net updates on the 963 grid,
approximately 182,178 GFLOPs. This corresponds to eight passes through the 19 support
scenes under the reference profile. The final recipes retain that numerical ceiling and
charge both calibration and optimization within it. Evaluation and recipe development
remain outside the budget. Medical and geological results therefore do not constitute an
equal-compute comparison across datasets.
   Calibration measures gradients on all 19 support scenes. Its strengths differ across
architectures, and the nnU-Net and UNETR settings follow exploratory comparisons.
Auxiliary terms enter through fixed ramps, with some signed-distance configurations

4.3.1   Testing Proposed Anatomical Rules

The MSD data descriptor [4] cites the manual annotation protocols of Pruessner et al.
[5] and Woolard and Heckers [6]. It identifies the last hippocampal-head slice as the
coronal slice containing the uncal apex; body and tail form the posterior label. Turning
this description into an automatic constraint requires a detectable landmark, a known
orientation and a relation that remains meaningful on the stored grid. The annotation
audit tests these operational assumptions before imposing them during learning.
   Two analyses address the proposed A/P transition rules. A planar analysis tests
whether one coronal separation reproduces the stored labels in all 260 crops. Coronal-
profile and image-cue analyses examine 52 original validation cases for a reliable way to
locate the transition.
   The image-cue investigation includes automated and visual searches but has no in-
dependently validated expert localization of the uncal apex. The audit can therefore
establish whether a tested numerical proxy fits the available labels. It cannot determine
whether the original annotators misapplied the anatomical protocol. Section 5.5.1 reports
the findings within the limitations and explains their consequences for constraint design.

4.5.2     Calibration and Activation

An auxiliary loss can dominate an update simply because its numerical scale differs from
the baseline. Calibration sets its coefficient by comparing the size of their gradients on
the labelled support cases. This uses training annotations only and holds the measured
network state fixed. The coefficients are then retained during optimization.
   The medical studies calibrate after a short baseline prefix: nine updates for nnU-Net
and 50 for the other models. In a combined objective, terms are calibrated sequentially
against the baseline and preceding terms. The order is bands, edges, signed distance
and depth. Consequently, a term’s coefficient can change when it is used in a different
combination; these experiments compare complete calibrated objectives. A median gradient
target sets the typical strength, while an upper-percentile bound limits unusually large

                                              33
contributions. The equations and numerical settings are given in Appendix A.7.
   Auxiliary terms enter gradually after the prefix. Signed distance either shares that
ramp or starts later, according to the fixed activation policy in Table 8. This policy
was developed from earlier MSD validation experiments and retained for the reported
campaigns; it was not selected independently of those exploratory results. Combinations
containing both signed distance and depth use delayed activation of the distance term.

4.5.3   Resource Accounting and Evaluation

The medical ceiling corresponds to the measured cost of 90 nnU-Net updates, approximately
31,976 billion floating-point operations (GFLOPs). Exploratory learning curves informed
this short reference allowance. With ten support crops and one crop per update, the
reference represents nine passes through the training subset. nnU-Net has the most
expensive update among the four implementations, so the same allowance lets models with
cheaper updates perform more optimization steps.
   Every medical model and objective receives that ceiling. The accounting includes the
forward pass, active training losses, backpropagation and the optimizer update. A step is
allowed only when its full cost fits in the remaining budget. Reference-target construction,
validation, data loading and storage are excluded, and calibration is recorded separately.
This matches optimization work, not elapsed time or end-to-end computation. Equal
allowances also need not place the architectures equally close to convergence.
   The reported checkpoint is the final state admitted by the budget, with no early stopping
or selection of a validation-best checkpoint. Selecting the highest-mean objective after the
experiments is a separate, descriptive comparison. All 504 runs in each medical study
enter the aggregation. Appendix A.8 gives the exact ceiling and arithmetic conventions;
Table 9 reports how the resulting update counts translate into passes through the data.
The same numerical medical ceiling is used on MSD and ADNI, while their different label
vocabularies remain part of the interpretation.

5.3    Error Analysis
Higher Dice can result from recovering more of an object, reducing foreground outside it,
or both. A false negative (FN) is foreground predicted as background; a false positive (FP)


                                             47
is background predicted as foreground. MSD additionally distinguishes anterior/posterior
swaps, which preserve whole-hippocampus membership but assign the wrong internal class.
These categories count different mistakes, so improved overlap does not imply that every
error type decreases.
   For the conventional geological models, the highest-mean nnU-Net objective reduces
both FN (34.4%) and FP (11.4%). SwinUNETR and the compact U-Net mainly reduce
FP, by 12.4% and 26.9%, while their FN counts rise slightly. UNETR shows the reverse
tradeoff: FN falls by 4.3% and FP rises by 2.9%. Thus, even within one dataset, spatial
supervision changes foreground retention and extension differently across architectures.
   The conventional medical results show a related pattern. On MSD, the selected nnU-
Net objective reduces missed foreground with a slight increase in extension; the other
models reduce extension while increasing omissions. All four selected configurations reduce
anterior/posterior swaps. On ADNI, nnU-Net reduces both binary error types, while the
other models exchange fewer false positives for more false negatives.
   Pretrained adaptation favours greater foreground recovery. SAM3’s highest-mean
objective reduces FN from 34.16 to 27.16 thousand voxels per volume, while FP rises from
3.70 to 6.99 thousand. Total incorrectly labelled voxels nevertheless fall by 9.8%. For
MedSAM3 on MSD, the selected objective reduces FN by 36.0% and internal swaps by
20.7%, while FP rises by 40.7%. On ADNI, FN falls by 24.0% and FP rises by 26.2%.
These changes explain why an overlap gain should be considered together with the error
balance relevant to the application.
   All counts cover the full evaluated image, including locations beyond the constraint
bands. Tables 19–24 in Appendix B.2 report every objective, including those with negative
Dice changes. Conventional-model counts are pooled within each run; adaptation counts
are means per image. Their absolute magnitudes therefore should not be compared across
studies. The error counts establish how much misclassification changes, but the retained
archives lack the paired prediction panels and surface measurements needed to establish
where those changes occur. The dataset illustrations show annotations, not evidence of
predicted segmentation quality.

5.5     Scope and Limitations of the Evidence
5.5.1   Annotation Compatibility and the Anterior/Posterior Transition

The audit in Section 4.3.1 examines whether the published anterior/posterior annotation
convention can support an additional anatomical constraint. It tests compatibility with
the released labels and the feasibility of locating the transition automatically.
   The MSD dataset description attributes manual tracing to two earlier hippocampal
annotation protocols [4]. Pruessner et al. [5] describe manual volumetry with simultaneous


                                             50
inspection of sagittal, coronal and horizontal views. Woolard and Heckers [6] build on that
protocol: sagittal tracing is refined in coronal views, and the hippocampus is then divided
into anterior uncus and posterior body and tail. Their anatomical cues include “more than
one cut through the hippocampus” in a coronal section and a medial protrusion towards
the crus cerebri; the division is checked in the sagittal view.
   The MSD paper specifies the final head section as the “coronal slice containing the
uncal apex” [4]. These descriptions motivate two candidate constraints: a single coronal
A/P separation and a transition located from the appearance or disappearance of the
folded uncal profile. The audit asks whether these descriptions yield reproducible rules
for the released crops. Exact separation on the stored voxel grid and disconnected mask
components are operational proxies; the papers describe an anatomical tracing procedure
using multiple views, not these numerical tests.
   The planar analysis finds that 58 of 260 crops cannot be reproduced exactly by a single
coronal A/P division. The best planes disagree with 1,388 of 856,754 foreground voxels
overall, approximately 0.162%. A strict universal rule therefore has counterexamples,
although the aggregate discrepancy is small. This supports treating the division as broadly
plane-like while retaining its actual discrete annotation for supervision.
   The profile analysis examines a numerical proxy for the multiple-cut cue described
by Woolard and Heckers [6]. Only two of 52 validation crops contain two disconnected
foreground components of at least two voxels each in the final anterior section; allowing
a window of three sections on either side of the fitted interface still identifies only two
crops. This criterion therefore does not recover the annotated transition consistently. A
folded three-dimensional structure can intersect one plane several times, and distinct tissue
profiles can remain connected in the discrete mask. Nor does the absence of a visible
separation prove the absence of the anatomical landmark. The explored image cues did
not establish a universally reliable automatic transition location.

5.5.2   Limitations of the Experimental Findings

The study evaluates spatial supervision across several architectures, medical and geological
datasets, and both training from scratch and pretrained-model adaptation. Matched
comparisons and repeated fold–seed evaluations characterize its contribution under the
specified experimental conditions. The scope of these findings is defined by the datasets,
training budgets and objective configurations examined.
   The medical experiments use prepared hippocampus crops, while the geological experi-
ments use synthetic volumes. Evaluation on additional anatomical structures, uncropped
images and measured seismic data would establish how broadly the observed behaviour
transfers. Similarly, the computational limits are an intentional part of the experimental
design: the results describe performance within those limits, while persistence of the gains
under substantially longer training remains to be assessed.
   The comparisons evaluate complete calibrated objectives. In particular, depth supervi-
sion combines additional class supervision with spatial weighting; a matched unweighted
control would isolate the contribution of the weighting itself.




                                            52
6     Conclusions
This thesis examined whether segmentation models can make better use of limited annota-
tions by explicitly supervising their spatial information. The LTN/logLTN formulation
connects four properties of the label maps to trainable losses: membership near boundaries,
agreement between neighbouring labels, classwise distance and supervision across depth
layers. These terms require no additional manual labels and leave the model’s inference
inputs unchanged.
    The experiments support this approach under the evaluated data and training budgets.
Benefits occur in both geological and medical tasks, and in models trained from scratch as
well as adapted pretrained models. Their size and the preferred objective depend on the
learning setting. The contribution is therefore a set of explicitly defined spatial objectives
and evidence of where they help, rather than a universally preferred combination. The
full comparisons, error tradeoffs and limits of this evidence are discussed in Chapter 5.


6.1    Future Research Directions
The demonstrated gains provide a foundation for extending spatial supervision to richer
anatomical tasks, broader imaging populations and more flexible adaptation strategies.
A central direction is to combine the general constraints developed here with knowledge
specific to the segmented structure. This would build on their ability to extract more
supervision from limited annotations while addressing properties that membership, local
transitions and distance weighting do not describe individually.
    Additional hippocampus datasets offer concrete opportunities for this extension. The
repository contains preparation code for the Montreal Neurological Institute hippocampal
subfield dataset (MNI-HISUB25), whose annotations distinguish the cornu ammonis (CA)
subfields CA1–3, the subicular complex and CA4–dentate gyrus [29], and for the CoBrA
hippocampal subfield atlas, which provides a finer subdivision including CA1, CA2/3 and a
separate label for the strata radiatum, lacunosum and moleculare [30]. Neither resource is
evaluated in this thesis. They would extend the investigation from whole-hippocampus and
A/P segmentation to smaller internal structures. The dataset of Jafari et al., containing
images of subjects with temporal lobe epilepsy and nonepileptic subjects, offers a further
extension to a different clinical population [31]; the repository currently contains an
inspection helper for this resource. Comparisons could retain each dataset’s native subfield
labels and assess transfer of whole-hippocampus supervision where foreground definitions
can be harmonized.
    These datasets would support families of anatomically specific constraints. Candidate
statements could describe which subfields share an interface, their relative positions


                                             53
within the hippocampus and the continuity of thin annotated layers. Shape constraints
could describe cross-sectional thickness and its variation along an individual hippocampal
long axis, while connectivity terms could discourage isolated predictions or interruptions
within a structure. The relations would be defined from the relevant annotation protocol
and expert knowledge, with soft tolerances that accommodate individual anatomy and
pathological variation. This would make it possible to test whether combining general
spatial supervision with structure-specific knowledge produces further gains, particularly
for small subregions with few annotated examples.
   The annotation audit also motivates a more flexible anatomical transition constraint.
Expert landmark annotations and multiplanar image context could support a confidence-
weighted transition region around the uncal apex. Such a formulation could represent
uncertainty in the A/P interface and follow the subject’s anatomy rather than imposing
a single fixed cut. Comparing landmark-guided supervision with the existing classwise
terms would examine the additional value of this anatomical information. The same
principle could guide constraints on other internal boundaries whose appearance varies
across subjects or imaging protocols.
   For pretrained models, a promising extension is to combine spatial supervision with
richer volumetric context and multiple image contrasts where available. Experiments could
vary adapter capacity, the layers being adapted and the number of neighbouring slices
processed together, while measuring the accuracy obtained for the added computation.
Varying support-set size and composition would show how the benefits scale with additional
annotation. An active-learning study could then use predictive uncertainty and anatomical
inconsistency to select informative volumes or regions for expert annotation, testing whether
targeted selection further reduces the amount of manual work needed for adaptation.
   The weighting of constraints could also become more responsive to the learning process.
Training-based estimates of class difficulty or target confidence could determine where and
when each term contributes most. Matched ablations of support geometry, normalization
and scheduling, including an unweighted classification control for depth supervision, would
identify which ingredients account for the gains and help simplify future recipes. Evaluating
several support sizes and compute allowances would map where each objective is most
useful, with matched data exposure as a complementary comparison. Independent studies
with choices fixed in advance and evaluation on new participants would reinforce the
evidence for these selected procedures across populations and training budgets.

A.8     Medical Resource Accounting
The medical budget was anchored to nnU-Net because its measured training update was
the most expensive among the four implementations. Exploratory learning curves informed
the choice of 90 nnU-Net updates as the minimum practical reference allowance for this
short-budget comparison. With ten training crops and one crop per update, this represents
nine complete passes through the selected training subset. The intention was to leave all
architectures a useful learning window while keeping computation limited. This choice
concerns computational cost, rather than model size alone: UNETR has more parameters
than nnU-Net, but cheaper updates in these configurations.
   The measured native nnU-Net baseline update costs 355,293,896,301 FLOPs. Multi-
plying it by the 90-update reference schedule gives the common optimization ceiling used
on both MSD and ADNI:

            Cmax = 31,976,450,667,090 FLOPs = 31,976.45066709 GFLOPs.                    (54)

Each other architecture receives this same FLOP allowance, with its number of updates
determined by its own measured cost. Cheaper models therefore receive more updates and
complete more epochs. The cap matches computational allowance across architectures; it
does not require them to reach the same stage of learning or convergence.
   The arithmetic ledger charges forward computation, active losses, backward compu-
tation and the optimizer update, counting two FLOPs per multiply–accumulate. An
update is admitted only if its charge fits within the remaining allowance. This defines the
optimization resource being compared; it does not measure GPU utilization or elapsed
time.
   Constraint calculations in the optimization graph are included. Construction of fixed
reference targets, validation, loading and storage are excluded. Calibration is accounted
separately. Thus, the matched quantity is optimization computation, while total training-
plus-calibration work can differ between objectives.
   Each dataset contributes 4 × 14 × 3 × 3 = 504 runs. The primary aggregate includes
every retained fold/seed cell, giving three seeds in each fold. The cap is the same numerical
allowance on both datasets, but their metrics concern different label vocabularies and
their starting difficulty need not be equal. Calibration remains additional work outside
this allowance.

A.9     Geological Budget and Schedules
The geological cap follows the same design principle as the medical comparison: start from a
short, practically useful update allowance for nnU-Net, the most computationally expensive
of the four implementations per update, and give every architecture the corresponding
FLOP allowance. This choice aims to allow all models to learn within a restricted budget
despite their different computational costs. Models with cheaper updates can consequently
make more passes through the training subset. The reference is update cost rather than
parameter count; these two quantities do not rank the architectures in the same order.
   For the 96 × 96 × 96 geological inputs, the reference allowance was 152 nnU-Net
updates, corresponding to eight complete passes through 19 training scenes. Multiplying
this count by the original Dice-only nnU-Net profile of 1,198.540306205 GFLOPs per
update established the numerical cap. The final comparison retains that cap and charges
both training and calibration computation within it:

                        Ctrain + Ccal ≤ 182,178.12654316 GFLOPs.                         (56)

Three-dimensional semantic segmentation identifies structures in an image
by assigning a class to every voxel, the three-dimensional counterpart
of a pixel. A stack of medical image slices can thereby become a map of
an organ; a geological image can become a map of a subsurface formation.
These maps make it possible to measure the size of a structure and study
its shape and relationship to its surroundings. Segmentation thus connects
image acquisition with quantitative analysis.

Traditionally, reference segmentations have been produced by delineating
structures in the images. A segmentation mask stores these decisions as
class labels on the image grid. In supervised learning, a neural network
learns to reproduce such masks from paired images and annotations.
Convolutional encoder--decoder models, notably U-Net and its volumetric
extension, made this approach widely applicable to biomedical images
\cite{ronneberger2015unet,cicek2016unet}. Transformer-based models offer
another way to combine information across an image, while pretrained
segmentation models allow an existing representation to be adapted to a
new task. These developments provide several routes to automatic
segmentation, but they do not remove two practical constraints:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Limited annotations.} Delineating a structure throughout a
volume takes time and can require specialist knowledge. In medical
imaging, uncertain boundaries further complicate consistent labelling
\cite{wang2021annotation}.
\item \textbf{Limited computation.} Training a volumetric model can be
expensive. An approach that reaches useful accuracy sooner may be
valuable even when a longer training run would eventually close the gap.
\end{itemize}

This thesis asks whether the spatial information already contained in a
mask can make learning more effective under these restrictions. Consider
a voxel just inside an annotated object: its label specifies membership,
but its location also tells us that it lies close to the object's edge.
Two neighbouring voxels reveal whether the annotation changes class at
that location. The proposed approach gives these relationships an explicit
role in the training objective, alongside the model's usual segmentation
loss. It needs no additional manual annotations.

Logic Tensor Networks (LTNs) provide a way to express such supervision
\cite{badreddine2022logic}. They belong to neuro-symbolic artificial
intelligence, which combines neural learning with explicitly stated
knowledge. In an LTN, a statement such as ``these annotated inner voxels
belong to the object'' receives a numerical degree of satisfaction from
the model's predictions. Training can then increase that satisfaction.
The logarithmic formulation, logLTN, represents truth values through
their logarithms and allows the corresponding penalties to be evaluated
in that space \cite{badreddine2023logltn}. Here, \emph{spatial constraints}
means soft training objectives: they encourage the stated properties
without guaranteeing them in every predicted segmentation.

Four forms of supervision are studied. The first reinforces membership
on either side of an annotated boundary. The second encourages
neighbouring predictions to reproduce the class transitions in the mask.
The third uses distance from each class boundary to weight membership
errors. The fourth balances class supervision across spatial layers of
the annotated region. The thesis connects each property to its logical
statement and numerical loss, then tests the terms individually and in
combination. This separates the formulation of a useful constraint from
the empirical question of whether it improves learning.

The investigation first evaluates these ideas on salt-body segmentation
in GeoFWI3D, a collection of synthetic subsurface models and associated
reflectivity volumes \cite{swaminadhan2026geofwi3d}. The foreground is the
salt region and the background is the surrounding material. This task
provides a nonmedical setting for testing spatial supervision before its
application to anatomical images.

The medical application is hippocampus segmentation in magnetic resonance
imaging (MRI). The hippocampus is involved in memory and is affected early
in Alzheimer's disease; identifying its boundaries supports measurements
of anatomical volume and variation \cite{niaAlzheimerBrain}. Two datasets
provide complementary tasks. The Medical Segmentation Decathlon (MSD)
distinguishes anterior and posterior hippocampus from background
\cite{simpson2019dataset}. It therefore requires both the outer contour
and an internal division to be recovered. The Alzheimer's Disease
Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) supplies images for a binary task,
distinguishing hippocampus from background in separate left and right
crops \cite{adniStudy}. Figure~\ref{fig:dataset-examples} illustrates the
images and masks used in these three settings.

The experiments address two learning situations. Models trained from
scratch must learn from the available task annotations, whereas pretrained
models start with representations learned from other images. The first
comparison includes convolutional and transformer architectures to test
whether the usefulness of spatial supervision depends on how a model
represents image context. The second adapts Segment Anything Model~3
(SAM3) to geological images and its medical specialization, MedSAM3, to
hippocampus images \cite{carion2025sam3,liu2025medsam3}. It asks whether
spatial supervision still helps when the initial representation is
already available. Within each study, constrained and baseline training
share the data and the stated computational or update budget.

The contribution is a common LTN/logLTN formulation of four spatial
objectives, together with an experimental assessment across these
architectures, datasets and learning situations. An additional audit
checks whether simplified geometric interpretations of the MSD
annotation protocol agree with the stored masks. The results examine
both the improvements and the settings in which an added term has little
benefit or reduces accuracy. Figure~\ref{fig:thesis-overview} summarizes
the design; the training schedules and replication details are given in
Chapter~\ref{sec:methodology}.
